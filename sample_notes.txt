Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae, and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy. This process takes place mainly in the leaves of plants, inside structures called chloroplasts. Chloroplasts contain a green pigment called chlorophyll, which absorbs sunlight. During photosynthesis, plants take in carbon dioxide from the air through small pores called stomata, and absorb water from the soil through their roots. Using light energy captured by chlorophyll, the plant converts carbon dioxide and water into glucose, a type of sugar that the plant uses for energy and growth. Oxygen is released as a byproduct of this reaction and is released into the atmosphere through the stomata. The overall chemical equation for photosynthesis is: six molecules of carbon dioxide plus six molecules of water, in the presence of light energy, produce one molecule of glucose plus six molecules of oxygen. Photosynthesis is critical for life on Earth because it produces the oxygen that most living organisms need to breathe, and it forms the base of almost all food chains, since plants are primary producers that other organisms depend on for energy.

Cellular respiration is the process by which cells break down glucose to release energy in the form of ATP, or adenosine triphosphate. This process happens in the mitochondria, often called the powerhouse of the cell. Cellular respiration uses oxygen and glucose as inputs and produces carbon dioxide, water, and energy as outputs, which makes it essentially the reverse of photosynthesis. There are three main stages of cellular respiration: glycolysis, which occurs in the cytoplasm and breaks glucose into pyruvate; the Krebs cycle, which occurs in the mitochondrial matrix and produces electron carriers; and the electron transport chain, which occurs on the inner mitochondrial membrane and produces the majority of the ATP. Cellular respiration is essential because ATP is the energy currency that powers nearly all cellular activities, from muscle contraction to protein synthesis.

The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms. There are two main types of cells: prokaryotic cells, which lack a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, and eukaryotic cells, which have a true nucleus enclosed by a nuclear membrane along with various specialized organelles. Animal and plant cells are both eukaryotic but differ in important ways. Plant cells have a rigid cell wall made of cellulose, a large central vacuole for storage, and chloroplasts for photosynthesis, none of which are present in animal cells. The nucleus of a eukaryotic cell contains the cell's genetic material, DNA, organized into chromosomes, and controls the cell's activities by regulating gene expression. The cell membrane, found in all cells, is a selectively permeable barrier made of a phospholipid bilayer that controls what substances enter and leave the cell.

DNA, or deoxyribonucleic acid, is the molecule that carries the genetic instructions used in the growth, development, functioning, and reproduction of all known living organisms. DNA is structured as a double helix, made up of two strands that wind around each other, connected by pairs of nucleotide bases: adenine pairs with thymine, and guanine pairs with cytosine. This complementary base pairing is what allows DNA to be accurately copied during cell division. The sequence of these four bases along a DNA strand encodes the genetic instructions needed to build and maintain an organism, much like letters in an alphabet spell out words and sentences. Genes are specific segments of DNA that contain the instructions for making particular proteins, and proteins in turn carry out most of the functions within cells.

An ecosystem is a community of living organisms interacting with each other and with their physical environment, including factors like sunlight, temperature, soil, and water. Ecosystems are typically organized into trophic levels based on how organisms obtain energy: producers, such as plants, which generate their own energy through photosynthesis; primary consumers, or herbivores, which eat producers; secondary consumers, or carnivores, which eat herbivores; and decomposers, such as fungi and bacteria, which break down dead organic matter and recycle nutrients back into the ecosystem. Energy flows through an ecosystem in one direction, typically depicted as a food chain or a more complex food web, while nutrients like carbon and nitrogen are cycled and reused repeatedly through biogeochemical cycles. A stable ecosystem depends on a balance between these different trophic levels; disrupting one level, for example by removing a top predator, can cause cascading effects throughout the entire food web.
